Daily MAPE trend by balancing authority

Mean absolute percentage error, one point per day. MAPE measures how noisy a forecast is. Published day-ahead MAPE for large US operators typically sits in the 1.5–4% band.

Accuracy vs bias

The distinction that matters. MAPE (bar) is noise — largely irreducible. Bias (line) is systematic direction, and it is the part a forecasting team can actually fix. A high-MAPE, zero-bias forecast is doing its best; a biased one is leaving money on the table every single day.

Overall vs peak-hour accuracy

Peak-hour accuracy is the number a capacity planner cares about. Capacity charges, reserve procurement and scarcity pricing all settle at the peak — being right on average is no comfort if you were wrong at the moment the system was most stressed.

Forecast bias vs cooling demand

The analytical centrepiece. Hours are bucketed by cooling degree hours (temperature above the 65°F baseline at which neither heating nor cooling is triggered). If the bars stayed near zero across all buckets the forecast would be unbiased. They do not — and that asymmetry is a fixable modelling gap, not bad luck.

Load vs temperature response

The characteristic U-shape: demand rises with heating below ~55°F and with cooling above ~70°F. The steepness of the right-hand arm is why hot-day forecasting is hard — a 2°F miss costs far more megawatts at 95°F than at 70°F.

Error concentration by day type

Holidays are structurally hard: load behaves like a Sunday even on a Tuesday, and a model has only ~10 examples per year to learn the pattern from.

Why this page can exist at all

The three data horizons are different lengths: actual demand publishes to about an hour ago, the day-ahead forecast reaches roughly 24–36 hours out, and the weather model reaches 72. Joining them with an INNER JOIN would keep only the overlap and silently discard the forward weather. A FULL OUTER JOIN plus an explicit row-type label keeps it, which turns a report about the past into a warning about tomorrow.

72-hour temperature outlook vs recent 30-day maximum

Bars above the zero line are hours forecast to be hotter than anything the system has been tested against in the last 30 days — conditions where the load model is extrapolating rather than interpolating, and where under-forecasting is historically worst.

Why a dashboard should show its own data quality

Every number above depends on assumptions that can silently break: an API can change its schema, a weather station can drop out, a refresh can fail at 3am. Publishing the checks alongside the results means a viewer never has to take the data on faith — and it answers the first question any competent reviewer asks, which is “how would you know if this were wrong?”

The business problem

Every US balancing authority publishes a day-ahead forecast of how much electricity its region will need, then buys power against it. When the forecast is too low the operator is short and must buy the shortfall on the real-time market, usually at a higher price. When it is too high the operator is long and sells the surplus back, often at a loss. Both directions cost money, and at 100 GW scale a 1% error is roughly 1,000 MW.

This dashboard grades those forecasts against what actually happened, then uses weather data to explain why they missed — separating irreducible noise from systematic, fixable bias.

Known limitations

Weather is a proxy. Each balancing authority is represented by a population-weighted blend of 4–6 major cities, not its true load-weighted weather. That understates variation in geographically sprawling footprints, MISO especially.
Correlation, not causation. A relationship between temperature and forecast error at hourly grain is suggestive. It does not establish that temperature modelling is the cause, and the wording throughout is hedged accordingly.
Revisions. EIA revises recent hours. The pipeline re-pulls a 10-day window on every run and upserts, so recent figures can move slightly after first publication.
No cost data. Real imbalance settlement prices are not free. Any dollar figure shown is an explicit user-set scenario assumption, never a measured result.
